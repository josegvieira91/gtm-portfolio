A target account list is cheap to get and expensive to trust. The names are
real, the firmographics are stale, and the first time a rep opens an account
with the wrong headcount on it, the whole list loses its credibility.
Enrichment fixes that, and it bills per answer, which is where the engineering
starts.

I built this on Clay's free plan: 100 data credits a month, 200 rows per table.
That budget shaped the decisions below more than any technical preference did.

## How it works

**Ordering the providers.** The list arrives from Google Sheets into a Clay
table, deduplicated on domain as it lands. Three legs then run in sequence.
Clay's own company enrichment goes first at one credit a row, a second provider
follows, and the company's own internal records answer last. In a paying account
I would flip that, because internal data is free and fresher than anything a
vendor sells. I kept the vendor-first order because the brief locked it, and the
cheaper ordering is the one I would argue for.

**What counts as a miss, and what a miss opens.** Clay's per-column run
condition would not persist for me, so I built the cascade into the inputs
instead. A formula column emits the domain only when the leg before it came back
empty, and the next leg reads that column as its required key. A leg with no key
never runs and cannot bill. The gate shows up in the table as a filled or blank
cell, which reads better than a setting buried in a side panel.

**Why a repeat costs nothing.** Auto-run stays on with "keep existing results"
checked, so only errored, empty or new cells ever fire. I re-ran the whole
waterfall over ten rows to check that claim: Clay's own confirmation dialog
quoted about 20 credits, and the counter moved by zero. Over the full 40-row run
the workbook spent 0 of its 100 credits. Clay charges for a successful
enrichment, and every domain on this list is a reserved example.com address that
no vendor can match.

**What the run proves, and what it does not.** The data rule for this portfolio
is synthetic companies only, so both vendor legs miss on every row by
construction. Twenty of the forty accounts were filled by the internal registry,
sixteen passed the ICP rule, and those sixteen reached HubSpot matched on domain,
so each write updated a company instead of creating a second one. The other
twenty-four kept a reason: not found, or found and out of profile. That is the
part worth defending. A list that quietly drops the accounts nobody could enrich
is worse than a short list, because you can no longer tell a gap from a decision.
